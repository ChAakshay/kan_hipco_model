NOVELTY	STATEMENT	(USE	THIS	AS	THE	CORE	CLAIM	OF	THE	PAPER)
We	present	the	first	end-to-end	machine-learning	decision	support	system	for	a	high-pressure	carbon	monoxide	(HiPCO)	single-walled
carbon	nanotube	reactor,	spanning	OPC-UA	historian	ingestion,	first-principles	feature	computation,	forward	quality	prediction,	and	inverse
recipe	recommendation.	To	overcome	extreme	batch-level	data	scarcity,	we	use	a	validated	167-formula	process	engine	as	a	physically
grounded	synthetic-data	generator	and	train	a	Kolmogorov-Arnold	Network	(KAN)	surrogate	whose	learnable	spline	activations	expose
process-to-quality	relationships	and	whose	differentiability	enables	gradient-based	inverse	optimization	of	setpoints	under	physics-feasibility
constraints.	We	benchmark	the	KAN	against	the	deployed	XGBoost	and	PLS	baselines	on	withheld	real	batches,	and	report	the
interpretability	structure	recovered	from	the	reactor.	The	contribution	is	not	a	new	learning	algorithm	but	a	validated,	deployable
integration	that	makes	differentiable,	interpretable	quality	control	tractable	for	a	proprietary,	low-data	industrial	nanomaterials	process.
Note	the	deliberate	hedging:	"first	end-to-end	DSS	for	a	HiPCO	reactor"	and	"not	a	new	learning	algorithm	but	a	validated	deployable	integration."	Both	are	true	and	both	are	hard	to
attack.	Do	not	upgrade	these	claims	to	"novel	KAN	architecture"	or	"novel	inverse	method."	You	will	lose	that	fight.

2.	Technical	roadmap	(current	system	to	KAN	architecture)
2.1	Forward	prediction	as	a	KAN
Keep	your	existing	feature	vector:	process	setpoints	plus	the	11	physics-engine	secondary	parameters	(flow	velocity,	heat	loss,	catalyst
concentration,	residence	time,	and	the	rest).	Targets	are	your	quality	metrics	(G/D	ratio,	UV-Vis	purity,	Fe/Ni/Cr	ppm	axial	and	radial).	Concrete
steps:
1.	
Use	the	
pykan
	reference	implementation	(or	an	efficient	variant	such	as	an	RBF-KAN	/	FastKAN,	or	Fourier-KAN)	so	training	stays	fast	on
your	small	data.	Start	with	a	shallow	network,	one	or	two	KAN	layers,	grid	size	3	to	5.	Deep	KANs	overfit	tiny	datasets.
2.	
Wrap	inputs	and	outputs	in	a	
StandardScaler
	pipeline	exactly	as	you	already	do,	so	the	KAN	and	the	baselines	see	identical	preprocessing.
This	keeps	the	comparison	honest.
3.	
Train	with	the	same	cross-validation	strategy	you	already	adapted	for	low	sample	count.	Report	CV	R2	and	NMAE	per	target,	matching	your
existing	metric	set,	so	numbers	are	comparable	across	models.
4.	
Apply	KAN	pruning	and	symbolic	snapping	(KAN	2.0	style)	after	training	to	extract	candidate	closed-form	activations.	These	become	your
interpretability	figures.
2.2	Gradient-based	inverse	optimization	through	the	KAN
This	is	where	the	KAN	earns	its	place	over	XGBoost.	Because	the	forward	KAN	
f(x)
	is	differentiable	end	to	end,	you	can	solve	for	setpoints	that
hit	a	desired	quality	target	by	gradient	descent	on	the	input:
Fix	the	trained	forward	KAN.	Define	a	loss	
L(x)	=	||	f(x)	-	y_target	||^2	+	lambda	*	penalty(x)
,	where	
penalty(x)
	enforces	box	bounds	and
physics	feasibility	(see	2.4).
Optimize	
x
	with	Adam	or	L-BFGS	from	several	physically	plausible	initial	points,	keeping	the	best	feasible	solution.	Multi-start	avoids	local
minima	and	gives	you	an	uncertainty	spread	on	the	recommendation.
This	directly	replaces	the	PLS	analytical	inverse	and	the	KNN-in-output-space	backtracker	with	a	single	principled	optimizer	that	covers	all
input	parameters	at	once,	which	is	exactly	the	direction	your	own	solver	iterations	were	already	heading.
2.3	Whether	and	how	to	ensemble	with	XGBoost	and	PLS
Do	not	drop	the	baselines.	Reframe	them	as	the	incumbents	you	must	beat	and	as	an	ensemble	option:
Primary	story:	KAN	as	the	deployable	single	model,
	with	XGBoost	and	PLS	as	baselines	in	your	comparison	table.	This	is	the	cleanest
narrative.
Optional	robustness	result:
	a	simple	average	or	stacked	ensemble	of	KAN	and	XGBoost	for	the	forward	task.	On	tiny	data,	an	ensemble
often	reduces	variance	and	gives	you	a	defensible	"best	forward	model"	number.	Report	it	as	an	ablation,	not	the	headline.
Keep	PLS	only	as	the	inverse	baseline
	so	you	can	show	the	differentiable	KAN	inverse	beating	the	linear	analytical	inverse	on	recipe
error.	That	contrast	is	a	good	figure.

3.	Synthetic	data	strategy
3.1	Build	the	dataset	from	your	engine	as	ground	truth
1.	
Define	the	input	sampling	space.
	For	each	real	setpoint	(CO	pressure,	catalyst	flow,	injector	geometry,	water	flow,	temperatures)	set	a
plausible	range	and	sample	with	Latin	Hypercube	or	Sobol	sampling	to	cover	the	space	evenly.	Do	not	sample	uniformly	and	independently	if
the	real	variables	are	correlated	(see	3.3).
2.	
Run	the	167-formula	engine	on	each	sampled	point
	to	compute	the	11	secondary	parameters.	This	gives	you	physically	consistent
(setpoint,	derived-parameter)	records	at	any	volume	you	want.
3.	
Generate	quality	targets
	either	from	a	physically	motivated	response	surface	you	fit	to	your	real	data	(kept	private)	or	by	training	an
intermediate	model	on	real	data	and	using	it	only	to	label	synthetic	points.	Disclose	which	you	used.	The	cleanest,	most	defensible	route	is	a
documented	parametric	response	model	with	literature-bounded	coefficients.
4.	
Never	publish	real	values.
	Report	only	ranges,	distributions,	and	normalized	quantities.	The	synthetic	set	is	what	appears	in	the	paper;	the
real	set	stays	behind	the	proprietary	wall	and	is	used	only	for	the	held-out	validation	numbers.
3.2	Literature	values	to	bound	the	ranges	(so	reviewers	accept	realism)
Anchor	your	synthetic	ranges	to	published	HiPCO	and	SWCNT	numbers	so	a	reviewer	cannot	say	"these	are	made	up."	Useful	anchors:	original
HiPCO	process	reports	(Nikolaev,	Smalley	and	co-workers)	for	CO	pressure	and	temperature	regimes;	SWCNT	G/D	ratios	typically	reported
from	roughly	2	up	to	the	tens	for	high-quality	material;	Raman-based	crystallinity	conventions;	and	the	aerosol-CVD	ML	datasets	(Nasibulin
group)	for	the	shape	of	parameter-to-quality	relationships.	Cite	these	when	you	state	each	range.	The	point	is	not	that	your	synthetic	values
equal	theirs,	it	is	that	they	fall	inside	physically	published	envelopes.
3.3	Make	the	data	not	"too	easy"
A	reviewer's	fastest	rejection	is	"the	synthetic	data	is	trivially	learnable."	Prevent	it:
Correlated	inputs.
	Impose	a	realistic	covariance	structure	(for	example	temperature	and	pressure	co-vary,	catalyst	flow	correlates	with
injector	geometry)	rather	than	independent	draws.	Sample	from	a	multivariate	distribution	with	an	estimated	correlation	matrix	(report	the
matrix	shape,	not	the	raw	data).
Heteroscedastic	noise.
	Add	measurement	noise	whose	magnitude	depends	on	the	operating	regime,	and	add	label	noise	on	the	quality
targets	sized	to	real	lab-measurement	repeatability	(G/D	ratio	and	ppm	values	have	non-trivial	measurement	error).	Justify	the	noise	levels
from	instrument	specs.
Missing-data	patterns.
	Mirror	the	real	gaps:	empty	Data	Logger	fields,	sensor	dropouts,	batches	missing	UV-Vis	or	ICP	results.	Inject	the
same	missingness	structure	so	the	model	is	tested	on	realistic	incompleteness.
Regime	imbalance.
	Real	production	clusters	around	a	few	recipes.	Make	the	synthetic	distribution	non-uniform	so	the	tails	are	sparse,
matching	reality	and	making	the	task	honestly	hard.
3.4	How	to	structure	the	paper's	data	section
Split	it	explicitly	into	two	subsections.	
Synthetic	methodology	(fully	disclosed):
	sampling	scheme,	engine	role,	range	table	with	literature
citations,	noise	and	missingness	models,	correlation	structure.	This	is	what	makes	the	work	reproducible	in	principle.	
Real-data	validation
(results	only,	no	values):
	state	sample	count	as	a	range	(for	example	"20	to	50	production	batches"),	describe	the	withheld-batch	protocol,
and	report	only	aggregate	metrics	(R2,	NMAE).	Add	one	sentence	stating	the	real	data	is	proprietary	and	cannot	be	released,	and	that	all
releasable	artifacts	are	synthetic.	Reviewers	accept	this	when	the	synthetic	methodology	is	thorough	enough	to	stand	alone.

5.2	Suggested	title	and	abstract
Title:
	"A	Physics-Augmented	Kolmogorov-Arnold	Decision	Support	System	for	Quality	Prediction	and	Inverse	Recipe	Recommendation	in	a
HiPCO	Carbon	Nanotube	Reactor."
DRAFT	ABSTRACT	(ABOUT	180	WORDS)
High-pressure	carbon	monoxide	(HiPCO)	synthesis	produces	high-quality	single-walled	carbon	nanotubes,	but	batch-to-batch	quality	is
difficult	to	predict	and	control,	and	production	data	is	scarce	and	proprietary.	We	present	an	end-to-end	decision	support	system	that
ingests	OPC-UA	reactor	historian	data,	reconstructs	production	state,	computes	secondary	process	parameters	through	a	validated	167-
formula	first-principles	engine,	and	predicts	nanotube	quality	(G/D	ratio,	optical	purity,	and	metal	impurity	levels).	To	address	extreme	data
scarcity,	we	use	the	process	engine	as	a	physically	grounded	synthetic-data	generator,	bounding	all	ranges	by	published	HiPCO	and	SWCNT
values	and	injecting	realistic	noise,	correlation,	and	missingness.	On	this	data	we	train	a	Kolmogorov-Arnold	Network	whose	learnable
spline	activations	expose	interpretable	process-to-quality	relationships	and	whose	differentiability	enables	gradient-based	inverse
optimization	of	setpoints	under	physics-feasibility	constraints.	Against	deployed	XGBoost	and	PLS	baselines	evaluated	on	withheld	real
batches,	the	system	matches	or	improves	forward	accuracy	and	yields	lower	inverse	recipe	error.	The	result	is	a	deployable,	interpretable,
and	differentiable	quality-control	tool	for	a	low-data	industrial	nanomaterials	process.
